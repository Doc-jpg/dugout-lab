# Dugout Lab research reference

## Purpose

This file is the evidence base behind Dugout Lab's practice planning, player development, drill variations, mental skills and Baseball IQ features. It summarizes what a few branded coaching products publicly advertise, separates documented features from marketing claims, then lists the published guidance we build on. Branded philosophies are optional perspectives; public health guidance and peer-reviewed findings carry more weight. Checked November 2, 2026. Nothing here reproduces paid course content, drill lists or video.

## Branded sources

### Hero Digitals (Baseball Coach Elite)

**What they publicly describe.** The site could not be read: the homepage blocks automated fetching and no indexed page or app-store listing turned up in search. Our brief says it advertises practice plans, drills, seasonal themes, age levels, rotating stations and roster management. Treat that as unverified until someone opens the site in a browser.

**Useful ideas for Dugout Lab.** The named feature set overlaps with ours: age bands, seasonal phases, station rotations, a roster that feeds plans. None of it is proprietary.

**Caveats.** Nothing confirmed. Do not cite this product as a model until the pages have been read.

### Dominate the Diamond

**What they publicly describe.** Plan bundles by age (up to 8U, up to 10U, 11-12U and 13+) tied to field sizes (46x60, 50x70, 60x90). Age bundles hold 6 plans with demo videos, progressing from first practice to opening day. Position-specific plans run 30 to 45 minutes as add-ons to a normal practice. Fall ball plans mention small-group stations, sign review, situational defense and team-building; pitcher and catcher plans mention arm care and pitch count awareness. The App Store listing describes a free iOS app with subscriptions ($49 monthly, $399 annual), over 1,000 videos, plans from 30 minutes to 2 hours, parent resources and lineup and evaluation tools, led by former pros Duke Baxter and Steve Nikorak of Zoned Sports Academy. The /baseball page returned an error and was not read.

**Useful ideas for Dugout Lab.** Age bands keyed to field size. Plans that progress across a preseason. Short position add-ons. Every drill linked to a demo. Fall ball as its own lighter, more game-like phase.

**Caveats.** "Runs like clockwork" and "decades of experience" are marketing. Testimonials are opinions. No outcome data. App Store rating was 3.1 from 23 ratings when checked.

### Bulletproof Hitter (Baseball Notes, Clint McGill)

**What they publicly describe.** A parent-facing mental skills program for youth hitters: six short video lessons, 24 recorded summit talks, printable confidence worksheets and a pre-game routine builder. Named topics are confidence, pressure, fear of failure, reacting to mistakes and pre-game routines. The core pitch is the player who looks good in practice and tightens up in games. Self-talk is not named. No age range is stated; testimonials mention kids around 10 to 14. One-time payment, 60-day refund. The creator is described as a former minor leaguer who later studied sport psychology.

**Useful ideas for Dugout Lab.** The problem statement (practice-to-game transfer) is the one coaches and parents feel. A routine builder and short repeatable lessons suit kids. Worksheets that put the child in charge of their own routine fit the self-controlled learning findings below.

**Caveats.** Urgency timers, "families helped" counts that disagree with each other (70K versus 75K), an unverifiable review score and a seller-written comparison against sports psychologists. A testimonial of .180 to .410 in three weeks is an anecdote. No independent evidence.

### Teacherman Hitting (Richard Schenck)

**What they publicly describe.** A six-week online program, High Level Pattern Hitting Secrets, with modules, group calls and a private community. Named concepts: a "high level pattern" said to be shared by elite hitters, two "pivot points" stretched against each other for a quick launch, a rear-legged hitter who controls the load rather than lunging, turning the barrel up to speed rather than pushing it, finding the right effort level and removing wasted motion, and a critique of cues like "knob to the ball." Rich Schenck says he has worked with MLB hitters including Aaron Judge. Loading and rotational sequence are not spelled out publicly.

**Useful ideas for Dugout Lab.** Rear-leg control and "don't lunge" are common ground with most instruction. Effort level as a dial, not always max, is useful for kids. Early barrel acceleration rather than pushing the hands is reasonable to show coaches as one perspective.

**Caveats.** Matching MLB swings in 30 days, or being more efficient than 99.9% of opponents, is marketing. The six-week program and 30-day promise do not match. No public evidence base. One branded model among several, not the way hitting works.

### Optimal Power Performance (AJ Arroyo)

**What they publicly describe.** A Phoenix-area training business with in-person and remote programming, downloadable guides, a coaching license program and a line of training bats, focused on hitting, throwing and strength from youth to pro. The founder's podcast describes the company starting in 2015 as a data-driven experiment. A youth episode for parents and coaches of 8 to 14 year olds covers the harm of early specialization, the value of multiple sports and building an athletic base first. The words in our brief (movement efficiency, assessment, coordination, speed, progression) do not appear on the homepage; treat them as a general description, not something the site documents.

**Useful ideas for Dugout Lab.** Assess before you prescribe. Athleticism and coordination first, then sport skill, then intensity. Multi-sport for 8 to 14 year olds. All of this matches the NSCA position below.

**Caveats.** "The only licensed training system in the country," scholarship counts and "trusted by D1 coaches" are marketing. No published outcome data found.

## Published guidance we build on

### MLB and USA Baseball Pitch Smart

The official guideline pages give a daily maximum and a required rest table. Confirmed from the MLB Pitch Smart pages:

| Age | Daily max | 0 days rest | 1 day | 2 days | 3 days | 4 days |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 7-8 | 50 | 1-20 | 21-35 | 36-50 | n/a | n/a |
| 9-10 | 75 | 1-20 | 21-35 | 36-50 | 51-65 | 66-75 |
| 11-12 | 85 | 1-20 | 21-35 | 36-50 | 51-65 | 66-85 |
| 13-14 | 95 | 1-20 | 21-35 | 36-50 | 51-65 | 66-95 |
| 15-16 | 95 | 1-30 | 31-45 | 46-60 | 61-75 | 76-95 |
| 17-18 | 105 | 1-30 | 31-45 | 46-60 | 61-80 | 81-105 |

Other rules on the age pages: no pitcher appears three days in a row regardless of count; at least four months off from throwing per year, two to three of them continuous; no more than 80 innings in 12 months for 9-12 and 100 for 13-14; fastball and changeup only through age 12, breaking pitches only after those are consistent; no pitching in two games on the same day; no catching on days you are not pitching; no playing for two teams at once; play other sports. For 9-12, a pitcher pulled from the mound may not return to pitch that game; for 13-14 a pitcher who stays in at another position may return once. Parents and players are responsible for tracking counts across all teams. League rules (Little League, tournaments) may differ; the app should default to Pitch Smart and let a coach pick a league table.

### NSCA long-term athletic development position statement

Ten pillars, condensed: individual and nonlinear development paths; fitness and psychosocial wellbeing for all youth; early focus on motor skills and strength; sport sampling over early specialization; child health as the central priority; conditioning that reduces injury risk; a range of training modes; monitoring and assessment; systematic, individualized programming; qualified coaches using sound teaching. Specific points: no single starting age for resistance training, but kids who can follow directions with good balance (around 6 to 7) can begin appropriate bodyweight and light-load work; growth spurts bring temporary clumsiness and may call for lighter loads; the statement warns against the "10,000 hours" idea, more than about 8 months a year in one sport, and about 16 training hours a week as a risk point; exertion as punishment is called abusive. Good coaching uses clear demos, short external cues, and treats failure as part of learning.

### Positive Coaching Alliance

Core ideas from PCA's public pages: the Double-Goal Coach (win, and teach life lessons); the ELM Tree of Mastery (Effort, Learning, Mistakes are OK); the Emotional Tank with a ratio of about five specific, truthful praises per correction; Honoring the Game (ROOTS: Rules, Opponents, Officials, Teammates, Self). PCA says research supports this but cites no studies on those pages. The ideas match broader findings on mastery-focused climates and are low risk to build in.

### Constraints-led coaching and representative design

The constraints-led approach shapes task, environment and performer constraints so the player discovers a solution rather than copying a model. Representative design means the practice task keeps the game's perception and action demands (live arm, real decision, real consequence). A systematic review of 30 studies on the approach for children's fundamental movement skills found promising but mixed results and asked for larger, longer studies. The 2019 Frontiers editorial on perceptual training says the same from the other side: strip out the real stimulus or the real movement and transfer to the field is limited.

### Blocked, variable and random practice

Contextual interference: mixing tasks or conditions usually lowers performance during practice and raises retention and transfer later. Two dials matter, task order (blocked to random) and within-task variability (constant to varied). The effect is strong in lab tasks and weaker in applied sport (Brady 2008); some field studies find no difference. Practical reading: start true beginners mostly blocked and constant; move to variable and random as the skill stabilizes; expect a dip in practice performance and do not read it as failure. For kids, short blocked stations that feed into game-like mixed reps in the same practice.

### Feedback frequency and motor learning

The guidance hypothesis holds that feedback on every rep can create dependence; reduced or faded schedules often help retention. Evidence is mixed. A 2010 study of 48 children (soccer throw-in) found the wording mattered more than the frequency: external-focus feedback (the ball, the target, the effect) at 100% frequency beat every other group, while body-part feedback was no better at high frequency and tended to be worse. The same literature supports self-controlled feedback (the athlete asks for it) and feedback after good trials. Working guidance: short external cues; after the first few reps of a new skill, drop to about one comment per three reps or on request; say what to do, not what went wrong; let the kid choose when to hear it.

### Decision-making and perceptual training

Expert anticipation comes from reading early cues and patterns. Screen training shows near transfer but limited far transfer unless it keeps the real movement and the real stimulus. One baseball study (Gray, cited in the 2019 editorial) found adaptive virtual-environment batting practice improved on-field batting the next season. Safe reading for a youth app: build Baseball IQ from short live-game situations and questions, pair any video with a real action, keep difficulty adaptive, treat screen-only drills as a supplement.

### Age-appropriate strength and coordination

Per the NSCA statement: early childhood is the window for a broad base of locomotor, object-control and balance skills plus foundational strength. Pre- and early-pubertal kids show large coordination gains from well-run resistance training. Technique and control first, load last; expect regressions during growth spurts.

### Confidence, imagery, self-talk and routines for kids

Adult meta-analyses show moderate benefits: mental practice around d = 0.5 (Driskell 1994); self-talk helps, with instructional cues for fine skills and motivational cues for effort tasks (Hatzigeorgiadis 2011, 32 studies); pre-performance routines help across ages and skill levels, with and without pressure (Rupprecht 2021, 800 athletes). Youth evidence is thinner and mixed: a study of gymnasts aged 7 to 15 found imagery helped only the more experienced kids, and imagery ability grows with age. Practical reading: routines and simple cue words are the safest tools for kids; imagery is a nice-to-have from about 11 up; everything is practiced in training before games.

## Principles we build on

- Pitch Smart is the default limit; rest days are enforced by pitch count, not innings (MLB Pitch Smart).
- Multi-sport and general athleticism first for 8 to 14 year olds; no more than about 8 months a year in baseball (NSCA).
- Strength means bodyweight, technique and coordination, progressed slowly, never as punishment (NSCA).
- Praise to correction around 5:1; effort, learning and mistakes are the practice scoreboard (PCA).
- Start skills blocked, then mix tasks and conditions in the same practice; expect a dip before the gain (contextual interference literature).
- Feedback is short, external-focus, less frequent as skill grows, and available on request (Wulf and colleagues).
- Practice tasks keep the real decision and the real action; screen-only training is a supplement (constraints-led and perceptual training literature).
- Routines and cue words at every age; imagery later, and practiced before games (Rupprecht 2021; Hatzigeorgiadis 2011; youth imagery study).
- Branded hitting or training models are shown as labeled perspectives with claims flagged, not as facts.

## What we will not do

- No copying paid course content, drill libraries, scripts or videos from any branded source. We write our own drills and credit ideas by name only.
- No mechanical diagnoses from video. The app prompts a coach's observation; it does not grade a swing or an arm.
- No weighted-ball, max-velocity or throwing-intent programs for kids. A six-week high school weighted-ball study reported a 24% injury rate in the training group versus none in controls, and youth elbow stress rises with ball weight (Reinold).
- No psychological labels. We track behaviors a coach can see (routine used, response after a mistake), not traits like "anxious."
- No public rankings, leaderboards or cross-family comparisons. Progress is visible to the player, parent and coach only.
- No pitching outside Pitch Smart limits, no breaking balls prescribed before 13, no catching on a pitching day.
